### [40] [Direct/adaptive-mixture phase-gradient learning for neural-network quantum states with complex phase structure](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13912)
*Yi-Ran Xue,Rui Wang,Baigeng Wang,Chenan Wei*

Main category: cond-mat.dis-nn

TL;DR: Neural-network quantum states (NQS) fail for complex phases (e.g., gauge fields, fermions) not due to network expressiveness but because the standard gradient estimator for the phase sector is a noisy score-function estimator. Replacing it with a direct estimator of the phase derivative of the local energy drastically reduces variance and improves accuracy, e.g., from ~1.8% to 0.89% median error on a 100-site flux ladder.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: NQS optimization is fragile when the ground state carries a non-trivial sign or complex phase structure (e.g., gauge fields, broken time-reversal symmetry, fermionic statistics). The cause was previously unclear—whether it was a network expressiveness issue or something else.

Method: The authors identify the stochastic score-function estimator of the phase gradient as the source of high variance. They propose a direct estimator that differentiates the local energy instead, requiring a separated amplitude–phase ansatz. They also introduce an adaptive-mixture of the two estimators with provably no worse variance than the better endpoint.

Result: On a 100-site flux ladder, the direct estimator achieves 0.89% median error vs. 1.8% for tuned baselines; wider/deeper networks with standard gradients degrade to 8.4%-24.6%. The advantage holds for chiral XXX chains, grows with flux, and vanishes in zero-flux controls. The adaptive mixture never underperforms the better endpoint.

Conclusion: Estimator design is a first-class lever for complex-valued neural quantum states; the direct estimator fixes the phase-gradient noise bottleneck without requiring more expressive networks.

Abstract: Neural-network quantum states (NQS) are a leading variational tool for quantum many-body physics, yet their optimization is fragile whenever the ground state carries a non-trivial sign or complex phase structure, a situation generic to gauge fields, broken time-reversal symmetry, and fermionic statistics. We trace this fragility to the stochastic estimator of the phase gradient rather than to network expressiveness. The phase sector of the Monte Carlo energy gradient is a noisy score-function estimator; differentiating the local energy instead yields a direct estimator that is unbiased for the same phase force, has far lower variance, and requires only a separated amplitude--phase ansatz. Demonstrated on a 100-site flux ladder, a small network trained this way reaches $0.89\%$ median error, where tuned standard baselines plateau at $1.8\%$ and wider or deeper standard-gradient networks degrade from $8.4\%$ to $24.6\%$. The advantage carries over to chiral XXX chains: the direct estimator again converges to a markedly lower error than the standard one, across $α$ and size; it grows with flux and vanishes in zero-flux controls. An adaptive-mixture of the two estimators is provably never worse in variance than the better endpoint at the optimal mixing coefficient, with seed-resolved diagnostics tracing much of the gain to eliminating failed runs. Estimator design thus emerges as a first-class lever for complex-valued neural quantum states.

### [41] [Local Coverage Governs Memorization in Diffusion Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14390)
*Claudia Merger,Sebastian Goldt*

Main category: cond-mat.dis-nn

TL;DR: Diffusion model memorization is governed by local data coverage: isolated training samples in low-density regions are memorized, while dense regions support generalization. This is derived via a connection to kernel density estimation and validated empirically.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Memorization in diffusion models was previously treated as a global property, but the observed coexistence of memorized and novel samples within a single model suggests it depends on local properties. The authors aim to identify which training samples are most likely to be memorized and provide a theoretical explanation.

Method: The authors leverage the connection between diffusion models and kernel density estimation (KDE) to derive a theoretical criterion predicting memorization based on local training data density and dataset size. They analyze the high-dimensional limit to identify a sharp local transition between memorization (low coverage) and interpolation/generalization (dense regions). Empirical validation is conducted, including multi-class settings where intra-class sparsity affects memorization.

Result: Memorization increases with local sparsity; diffusion models exhibit coexistence of memorized and novel samples. In multi-class settings, classes with higher intra-class sparsity (lower local coverage) are more strongly memorized. The theoretical predictions are empirically validated.

Conclusion: Memorization in diffusion models is a local phenomenon governed by data geometry and local coverage. The framework explains when and where memorization occurs, providing a local view that bridges memorization and generalization.

Abstract: Memorization in diffusion models is often treated as a global property of the model or dataset. In practice, however, a single diffusion model can simultaneously generate both memorized and novel samples. Which training samples are most likely to be memorized? In this work, we show that memorization is governed by \emph{local data coverage}. Leveraging the connection between diffusion models and kernel density estimation (KDE), we derive a theoretical criterion that predicts whether a point is memorized based on the density of training data in its neighborhood and the size of the training dataset. In the high-dimensional limit, this leads to a sharp, local transition: regions of low coverage are dominated by isolated training samples, which are memorized, while dense regions support interpolation and generalization. We validate these predictions empirically, showing that memorization increases with local sparsity and that diffusion models exhibit a coexistence of memorized and novel samples within the same model. Extending this framework to multi-class settings, we further show that classes with higher intra-class sparsity (and thus lower local coverage) are more strongly memorized. Our results provide a local view of memorization in diffusion models, explaining when and where memorization occurs in terms of data geometry.

</details>
