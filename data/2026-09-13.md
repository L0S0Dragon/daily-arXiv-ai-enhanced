### [1] [Learning structural balance of graphs from quantum spectral features](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11736)
*Stefano Scali,Oleksandr Kyriienko*

Main category: quant-ph

TL;DR: This paper introduces a quantum method for spectral feature extraction from the density of states (DOS) of a problem-dependent Hamiltonian and applies it to learning on signed graphs, using standardized moments of the Ising DOS as switching-invariant, size-free features that count signed closed walks.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: The paper is motivated by the need for quantum approaches that extract useful spectral features from many-body Hamiltonians and apply them to machine learning problems such as signed graph analysis, including the NP-hard frustration index, where exact ground truth is available at moderate sizes for certification.

Method: The authors embed a signed graph as an Ising model with positive and negative interactions, use standardized moments of the Ising DOS as features, and propose DOS-QPE, a phase estimation procedure on a purified maximally mixed probe, to sample the spectral density with far fewer shots than Hadamard test-based trace sampling.

Result: On 1.4×10^5 labeled graphs, the exact DOS determines the frustration index, and five moments recover it with a mean error of 0.4, well below one sign flip; at zero field the models can be sampled classically for exact certification, while beyond zero field the trace-estimation problem is DQC1-complete.

Conclusion: The work opens routes toward quantum applications in social network balance analysis, spin-glass studies, correlation clustering, and protein-interaction networks.

Abstract: We develop a quantum approach to spectral feature extraction from the density of states (DOS) of a problem-dependent Hamiltonian, and apply it to machine learning on signed graphs. We propose to embed a signed graph as an Ising model instance with positive and negative interactions, and use the standardized moments of the Ising DOS as features for learning. We show that these moments count signed closed walks, are switching-invariant, and are size-free by construction. As a benchmark, we target learning the frustration index, an NP-hard measure of structural balance that can be labeled exactly at moderate size. At zero field, the models can be sampled classically, allowing the quantum extraction procedure to be certified against exact ground truth. We propose DOS-QPE, a phase estimation on a purified maximally mixed probe, which samples the spectral density with orders of magnitude fewer shots than Hadamard test-based trace sampling and feeds the resulting features directly into classically trained models. On $1.4\times10^5$ labeled graphs the exact DOS determines the frustration index, and five moments recover it with a mean error of 0.4, well below one sign flip. Beyond zero field, the underlying trace-estimation problem is DQC1-complete, providing access to spectral features for which no efficient classical sampling method is known. Our work opens routes towards quantum applications in social network balance analysis, spin-glass studies, correlation clustering, and protein-interaction networks.

### [2] [Quantum Inversion of Units in Group Rings: Block Dimension, Not Commutativity, Governs Hardness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10596)
*Bhanwar Gupta*

Main category: quant-ph

TL;DR: Unit inversion in group rings—including dihedral group rings used to revive group-ring cryptosystems—is polynomial-time solvable classically and quantumly via a basis change into small matrix blocks, so it is not protected by dihedral HSP hardness.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Public-key schemes rely on the assumed hardness of inverting a unit in a group ring. A prior result broke this assumption for abelian groups on quantum computers, so designers shifted to non-abelian groups, particularly dihedral groups, assuming the dihedral hidden subgroup problem (HSP) would provide security. This paper argues that unit inversion is a distinct problem that does not require an HSP solver and therefore is not shielded by dihedral HSP hardness.

Method: The approach reformulates unit inversion as a basis change that decomposes (splits) the group ring into small matrix blocks, rather than solving an HSP. The authors prove polynomial-time solvability, both classically and quantumly, under three conditions: an efficient generalized Fourier transform exists, the group ring is semisimple, and the largest matrix block has polynomial size. They show dihedral group rings meet these conditions because their irreducible representations have dimension at most 2 and an efficient Fourier transform exists. They provide an explicit reversible quantum circuit for the block-inversion step, validate it in a register-level simulator, and identify the exact structural boundary where the method ceases to apply.

Result: Unit inversion is proven polynomial-time classically and quantumly for the stated conditions, and dihedral group rings satisfy them, contradicting the security rationale of dihedral-group-based schemes. A reversible quantum circuit for block inversion is given and confirmed via register-level simulation. The precise structural boundary of the technique is characterized, and a candidate construction in the surviving regime is proposed under a new, clearly stated security assumption. The constructive results are backed by reproducible software artifacts and experiments.

Conclusion: Unit inversion is a separate problem from the dihedral HSP, so hardness of the latter does not protect group-ring cryptosystems. Whenever an efficient generalized Fourier transform exists, the ring is semisimple, and matrix blocks are polynomially sized, unit inversion is efficiently breakable; dihedral groups fall in this regime. Beyond this boundary, a new security assumption is needed, for which the paper offers a candidate construction.

Abstract: Several public-key schemes base their security on the belief that inverting a unit of a group ring is hard. A recent result showed that this belief is false on a quantum computer when the group is abelian. To restore security, designers moved to non-abelian groups, especially dihedral groups, believing that the hardness of the dihedral hidden subgroup problem (HSP) would protect the scheme. This paper shows that unit inversion is a different problem and does not require an HSP solver. Instead, it can be solved by a change of basis that splits the group ring into small matrix blocks. We prove that unit inversion is polynomial-time, classically and quantumly, when an efficient generalized Fourier transform exists, the group ring is semisimple, and the largest matrix block has polynomial size. Dihedral group rings satisfy these conditions because their irreducible representations have dimension at most 2 and an efficient Fourier transform exists. We give an explicit reversible quantum circuit for the block-inversion step and validate it in a register-level simulator. We also identify the exact structural boundary where the method stops and propose a candidate construction in the surviving regime under a new, clearly stated security assumption. The constructive results are supported by reproducible software artifacts and experiments.

### [3] [Sample-optimal learning of stabilizer states](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10974)
*Rebecca Chang,Matthias C. Caro,Martin Larocca,Maxwell West*

Main category: quant-ph

TL;DR: The paper tightly determines the constant coefficient in the copy complexity of learning stabilizer states, proving $n+\lceil\log_2(1/\delta)\rceil-3 \le L_\delta(n) \le n+\lceil\log_2(1/\delta)\rceil+4$, with a polynomial-time algorithm saturating the bound.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Prior work established that learning a pure $n$-qubit stabilizer state requires, and can be achieved with, a number of copies linear in $n$, but the exact constant coefficient of this linear scaling had not been determined. The authors aim to pin down this constant precisely and improve sample complexity over previously known approaches.

Method: The authors use Fourier analysis on the abelian group $\mathbb{Z}_4^n \times \mathbb{F}_2^{n(n-1)/2}$, a technique claimed to be qualitatively different from prior stabilizer-state-learning approaches. They design a polynomial-time quantum learning algorithm that saturates the derived lower bound, and use the Choi-Jamiolkowski isomorphism to extend the result to learning Clifford unitaries.

Result: They prove $n+\lceil\log_2(1/\delta)\rceil-3 \leq L_\delta(n)\leq n+\left\lceil\log_2(1/\delta)\right\rceil+4$ for the minimum copies needed to identify any stabilizer state with failure probability at most $\delta$ ($0<\delta<1/8$). Their polynomial-time algorithm saturates this bound, giving a constant-factor sample-complexity improvement. As a corollary, they obtain an algorithm learning an unknown $n$-qubit Clifford unitary from $2n+\lceil\log_2(1/\delta)\rceil+4$ queries, whose $n$-dependence is optimal.

Conclusion: The constant coefficient of the linear copy complexity of stabilizer-state learning is essentially determined, and an optimal (in $n$-dependence) Clifford-unitary learning algorithm follows. The Fourier-analytic proof technique is qualitatively novel and admits natural generalizations to other quantum learning problems.

Abstract: It is well-known that learning a pure $n$-qubit stabilizer state $|ψ\rangle$ both requires, and can be accomplished with, access to a number of copies of $|ψ\rangle$ linear in $n$. However, the precise constant coefficient of this scaling does not appear to have been determined. Here we prove that $L_δ(n)$, the smallest number of copies from which a quantum procedure can identify any stabilizer state with failure probability at most $0<δ<1/8$, satisfies $n+\lceil\log_2(1/δ)\rceil-3\leq L_δ(n)\leq n+\left\lceil\log_2(1/δ)\right\rceil+4$. We present a polynomial-time quantum learning algorithm that saturates this bound, achieving a constant factor improvement in sample-complexity over previously known approaches. As an immediate corollary, we obtain via the Choi-Jamiolkowski isomorphism an algorithm for learning an unknown $n$-qubit Clifford unitary from $2n+\left\lceil\log_2(1/δ)\right\rceil+4$ queries, the $n$-dependence of which we show to be optimal. Our proof technique, which involves Fourier analysis on the abelian group $\mathbb{Z}_4^n \times \mathbb{F}_2^{n(n-1)/2}$, seems to be qualitatively different to previous approaches to stabilizer state learning, and may be of some independent interest; in particular, it admits natural generalisations to further problems in quantum learning theory.

### [4] [Coherent Floquet quantum reservoirs for molecular property prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11071)
*Luofei Wang,Da Zhang,Congren Wang,Yiming Li,Yuxiao Yang,Xuan Zhang,Xuefeng Cui,Zhang-Qi Yin*

Main category: quant-ph

TL;DR: The paper builds a discrete time crystal (DTC)-based quantum reservoir computing architecture that predicts molecular properties from graph and dynamical inputs, outperforming echo-state networks on long-prefix graph classification and ethene gap forecasting.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Quantum reservoir computing can encode input histories via quantum dynamics and decode them with a fixed classical readout. DTCs show robust subharmonic responses under periodic driving, and prior DTC-QRC work motivates using this physics to process structured molecular data for property prediction.

Method: A DTC-based reservoir architecture processes local molecular graph events and surface-hopping frames through coherent Floquet evolution; controlled reset regulates earlier-input contribution. End-of-sequence measurements yield a fixed-dimension feature vector. Trained classical decoders perform inhibitor-activity and blood-brain-barrier permeability classification and electronic-gap forecasting, while reservoir parameters stay fixed during training.

Result: With matched input lengths and output widths, DTC-QRC outperforms echo-state networks on long-prefix graph classification and the studied ethene gap forecasting. Dephasing lowers performance in both tasks, indicating coherent propagation matters. On the Quafu superconducting quantum platform, pair observables retain task information under device noise.

Conclusion: The architecture offers a common framework for molecular screening and time-resolved property prediction via quantum reservoir computing.

Abstract: Quantum reservoir computing (QRC) uses quantum dynamics to represent input histories for prediction through a trained classical readout. Discrete time crystals (DTCs) exhibit robust subharmonic responses under periodic driving, and previous work has used their dynamics to construct DTC-QRC. Here we construct a DTC-based reservoir architecture to predict molecular properties from structural and dynamical observations. Coherent Floquet evolution processes local molecular graph events and surface-hopping frames, while controlled reset regulates the contribution of earlier inputs. Measurements at the end of each input sequence yield a feature vector of fixed dimension. Trained classical decoders use this vector for inhibitor-activity and blood--brain-barrier permeability classification and electronic-gap forecasting, while the reservoir parameters remain fixed during training. With matched input lengths and output widths, DTC-QRC outperforms echo-state networks on long-prefix graph classification and the studied ethene gap forecasting tasks. Dephasing lowers performance in both applications, consistent with a role for coherent propagation. Experiments on the Quafu superconducting quantum cloud platform show that pair observables retain task information under device noise. The architecture provides a common framework for molecular screening and time-resolved property prediction using quantum reservoir computing.

### [5] [Nonlinear dynamics and mechanical frequency combs with a Meissner-levitated micromagnet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11668)
*Yiqi Wang,Trisha Madhavan,Vanessa Wachter,J. DaLi Schaefer,Addison NewRingeisen,Zhiyuan Wei,Frankie Fung,Benjamin A. Stickler,Mikhail D. Lukin*

Main category: quant-ph

TL;DR: A Meissner-levitated micromagnet is shown to be a versatile nonlinear multimode mechanical platform, enabling tunable phononic frequency combs and phase-locked librational dynamics.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Nonlinearities in multimode mechanical systems enable rich dynamics useful for sensing and quantum experiments, so a platform combining low dissipation, tunability, and strong nonlinearities is desirable.

Method: The coupled center-of-mass and rotational motion of a levitated micromagnet is exploited; parametric excitation of two translational modes drives cascaded nonlinear frequency mixing, and nonlinear intermodal coupling to a low-frequency librational mode is studied at large amplitudes.

Result: A mechanical (phononic) frequency comb with tunable spacing is generated. At large amplitudes, nonlinear coupling parametrically excites and phase-locks the librational mode, producing a dense spectral fine structure at subharmonics of the drive.

Conclusion: Levitated micromagnets constitute a promising platform for nonlinear multimode mechanics, with potential for precision sensing and quantum-limited metrology.

Abstract: Nonlinearities in multimode mechanical systems can give rise to rich dynamical phenomena with great potential for sensing applications and for future quantum experiments. We demonstrate that the coupled center-of-mass and rotational motion of a Meissner-levitated micromagnet offer a promising platform for nonlinear dynamics, combining low dissipation, magnetic tunability, strong intrinsic Duffing nonlinearities, and nonlinear intermodal couplings. We use this tunability to demonstrate the generation of a mechanical frequency comb in the micromagnet dynamics, realized by parametric excitation of two translational modes followed by cascaded nonlinear frequency mixing, which produces a phononic comb with tunable spacing. At large amplitudes, nonlinear coupling to a low-frequency librational mode of the magnet leads to parametric excitation and phase locking of that mode, generating a dense spectral fine structure at subharmonics of the drive. These results establish levitated micromagnets as a platform for nonlinear multimode mechanics, with potential applications in precision sensing and quantum-limited metrology.
